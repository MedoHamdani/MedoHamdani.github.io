Flatfoot and Knee Pain: How Flat Feet Affect Your Knees and Treatment

A detailed, evidence-based guide on the link between flatfoot (pes planus) and knee problems, what causes them, and how to treat flat feet effectively.

Health & Movement • Reading time: 12 min

1. What Is Flatfoot?

Flatfoot, medically called pes planus, is a condition where the arch of the foot collapses and the entire sole comes into contact with the ground. While many people are born with flexible flat feet, the condition can also develop later in life as the arch drops due to weakened tendons, injury, or aging.

Key point: Flat feet often cause the foot to roll inward too much. This motion is called overpronation, and it changes how forces travel through the whole leg, all the way to the knee.

Types of Flat Feet

  • Flexible flatfoot: The arch appears only when the foot is lifted off the ground.
  • Rigid flatfoot: The arch is always flat, usually painful and may need medical treatment.
  • Acquired flatfoot (adult-acquired): Develops later in life, often from posterior tibial tendon dysfunction.

2. What Is the Knee and How Does It Work?

The knee is a hinge joint formed by the femur (thigh bone), tibia (shin bone), and the patella (kneecap). It relies on ligaments, cartilage, muscles, and tendons for stability. The alignment and movement of the knee are highly dependent on the position of the foot and ankle — especially the arch.

Healthy knee structure depends heavily on proper foot alignment.

3. The Link Between Flat Feet and Knee Pain

When the foot arch drops and the foot overpronates, the tibia rotates inward. This inward rotation changes the mechanical alignment at the knee joint. Over time, the misalignment increases stress on the cartilage and soft tissues around the knee — especially on the medial (inner) side.

Medical research supports this link. A study published in Arthritis Care & Research found that flatfoot is associated with frequent knee pain and medial tibiofemoral cartilage damage in older adults. Other studies found an increased risk of anterior knee pain and reduced knee joint stability in people with pronounced overpronation.

3.1 How the Mechanics Work

  1. Flat foot collapses → arch lowers
  2. Overpronation increases → ankle rolls inward
  3. Internal tibial rotation → shin bone twists inward
  4. Knee valgus stress → knee moves inward under load
  5. Cartilage & ligament stress → pain, stiffness, and gradual wear

4. Common Symptoms

  • Pain around the inner knee or along the joint line
  • Swelling after walking, running, or standing for long periods
  • A feeling that the knee is “giving way” or buckling
  • Increased pain during stairs, squatting, or impact activities
  • Visible flattening of the foot arch when standing
  • Shoes wearing out quickly on the inner side

5. How Is Flatfoot Diagnosed?

Diagnosis is usually based on a physical exam, gait analysis, and sometimes imaging:

  • Visual and mobility exam: checking arch height while sitting, standing, and on tiptoes.
  • Wet test: footprint analysis to see arch shape.
  • X-ray, CT, or MRI: to check bone and cartilage changes if needed.

6. Treatment Options for Flat Feet and Related Knee Pain

6.1 Conservative / Non-Surgical Treatments

Most cases respond well to conservative care, especially when started early.

Foot Orthotics and Arch Supports

Custom or prefabricated orthotics help support the arch, control pronation, and improve lower limb alignment. This reduces torque on the knee and decreases pain during daily activities.

Physical Therapy Exercises

  • Calf and Achilles stretches
  • Short foot exercises to activate the arch muscles
  • Toe scrunches and towel pickups
  • Single-leg balance work
  • Hip and core strengthening to control knee valgus

Footwear Guidance

Stability or motion-control shoes with good arch support and heel cushioning are often recommended. Avoid completely flat or highly worn shoes.

Weight Management and Activity Modification

Reducing repetitive high-impact activities while strength improves can prevent flare-ups. Swimming, cycling, and walking on softer surfaces are good alternatives.

6.2 Medical and Surgical Options

If pain persists despite conservative treatment, a doctor may consider:

  • Anti-inflammatory medications
  • Physical therapy guided by a physiotherapist
  • Injection therapies in some cases
  • Surgery in severe rigid flatfoot or when there is tendon rupture or significant arthritis
Note: Always consult a podiatrist, orthopedist, or physiotherapist for a personalized treatment plan. This article is educational and not a substitute for professional medical advice.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Can flat feet cause permanent knee damage?
Not necessarily. Many people manage symptoms well with orthotics, exercises, and proper footwear. However, longstanding untreated severe overpronation may increase the risk of joint wear over time.

Is surgery the only cure for flatfoot?
No. Most flexible flatfeet are treated without surgery. Surgery is usually reserved for rigid or painful structural deformities.

Do arch supports really help knee pain?
Yes. By restoring better foot alignment, orthotics can reduce abnormal knee loading and reduce pain in many patients.

8. Prevention Tips

  1. Wear supportive shoes with good arch structure.
  2. Do foot-strengthening exercises regularly.
  3. Avoid prolonged standing in flat shoes.
  4. Maintain a healthy weight.
  5. Address foot pain early before compensations spread to the knee.
