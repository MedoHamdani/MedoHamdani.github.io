Flat Feet and Knee Pain: The Hidden Connection and How to Fix It

Understanding fallen arches (pes planus), how they affect your knees, and evidence-based exercises to strengthen your feet and reduce pain.

Normal arch vs flat foot — how the medial longitudinal arch collapses in pes planus.

What Are Flat Feet?

Flat feet — also called fallen arches or pes planus — is a common condition where the arch of the foot collapses, causing the entire sole to touch the ground. While many people have flat feet without pain, others develop a chain reaction of problems that travels upward: from the ankles to the knees, hips, and lower back.

Key point: Flat feet don’t just affect the feet. When the arch collapses, it changes how forces travel through the entire kinetic chain — often creating knee valgus, hip Drop and lower back stress.

How Flat Feet Affect the Knee

When the arch collapses, the foot rolls inward excessively — this is called overpronation. This inward rolling motion travels up the tibia and affects knee alignment. The knee may drift inward, creating medial knee stress.

Pes planus: the medial longitudinal arch contacts the ground during stance phase.

This can lead to:

  • Medial knee pain — pressure on the inner knee joint
  • Patellofemoral pain — pain around or behind the kneecap
  • Meniscus irritation — increased shear forces in the knee
  • Hip and back pain — compensation patterns above the knee

Understanding the Foot Muscles: Intrinsic vs Extrinsic

To treat flat feet effectively, you need to train both muscle systems that control the foot.

Intrinsic Foot Muscles

These are the small muscles inside the foot itself that support the arch from within. They create fine arch control and stability.

  • Located entirely within the foot
  • Support the arch during balance and gait
  • Best trained with short foot exercises, toe spreading, and ball pick-ups

Extrinsic Foot Muscles

These are the larger muscles in the lower leg that attach to the foot via tendons. They provide the main power and deceleration.

  • Tibialis Anterior: lifts the inner arch and controls foot lowering
  • Calf muscles: Gastroc/Soleus — control ankle stability
  • Peroneal tendons: provide outer foot stability
  • Flexor Hallucis Longus / Tibialis Posterior: deep dynamic arch support

Treatment: Intrinsic Foot Muscle Exercises

These exercises target the small stabilizers inside the foot. Use a soft, smooth surface for towel exercises so the towel can slide easily. Carpet makes this harder.

1. Towel Scrunches

Target: Intrinsic foot muscles, arch support

  1. Sit on a chair with a soft towel on a smooth floor (tiles or wood)
  2. Place your foot on the towel
  3. Use your toes to scrunch the towel toward you
  4. Release and repeat 10–15 times per foot

2. Marble or Hacky Sack Pick-Up

Pick up small objects with your toes to improve arch control and toe dexterity.

Target: Intrinsics + toe coordination

  1. Place marbles, small objects, or a hacky sack on the floor
  2. Pick them up using only your toes
  3. Place into a cup or container
  4. The hacky sack method is more controlled and fun than towels

3. Toe Separators

Toe separators help restore natural toe splay so the foot muscles can function properly.

Target: Metatarsal arch, toe splay

  1. Use Correct Toes or similar toe spacers
  2. Start with 15–30 minutes daily
  3. Progress to wearing them during exercises and walking
  4. Goal: comfortable 1-hour wear, even while jogging

Treatment: Extrinsic Foot Muscle Exercises

These larger lower-leg controls are essential for deceleration and arch lift during gait.

1. Tibialis Raises

Target: Tibialis Anterior, dynamic arch lift

  1. Stand with feet hip-width apart
  2. Keep toes and outer foot flat on the ground
  3. Raise the inner arch as high as possible
  4. Hold 2 seconds, lower slowly
  5. 1–2 sets daily, 15–20 reps per leg

2. Calf Raises

Target: Gastrocnemius, Soleus, overall foot stability

  1. Beginner: Both feet, hands on wall
  2. Intermediate: Single leg, 20 reps
  3. Advanced: Farther from wall, single leg, hold added weight

Build to two sets daily. These exercises absorb landing forces and protect the knee.

Footwear, Orthotics and Common Mistakes

Supportive shoes with good arch support are essential — but orthotics alone are not enough.
  • Orthotics provide short-term support and pain relief
  • Supportive shoes prevent the foot from collapsing further
  • Mistake: Relying only on orthotics. This can make foot muscles weaker over time
  • Fix: Use orthotics as a bridge while you strengthen the foot

Lifestyle Modifications

  1. Maintain a healthy weight — reduces load on the arches
  2. Stretch calves and hamstrings — tight posterior chain forces the foot to flatten
  3. Rest and ice after painful activity
  4. Gradual progression — don’t jump into high volume too fast

When to See a Doctor

If you experience any of the following, consult a healthcare professional:

  • Severe or persistent knee pain
  • Sudden collapse of the arch in adulthood
  • Pain limiting daily activities or sports
  • Doctors may recommend imaging X-rays, MRI, or custom orthotics

Conclusion

Flat feet are common, but they’re manageable. The most effective approach combines:

  1. Intrinsic exercises — towel scrunches, marble pick-ups, toe separators
  2. Extrinsic exercises — Tibialis raises, calf raises
  3. Supportive footwear — arch support without permanent reliance on orthotics
  4. Gradual progression — build reps and duration safely

If your knee is painful, strengthen it with single-leg controlled exercises. If a doctor mentioned meniscus issues, they likely mean targeted rehab — not avoidance. You can build a proper program alongside your foot rehab.

Consistency beats intensity. Even 10 minutes of foot exercises daily will improve arch control over weeks and months.
