Flat Feet and Knee Pain: The Hidden Connection

How fallen arches affect your knees — and what you can do about it

Quick Summary: Flat feet (pes planus) change the way forces travel up your legs. Without proper arch support, your knees absorb extra stress, which can lead to pain, instability, and long-term joint wear. The good news: targeted exercises, proper footwear, and orthotics can help.

What Are Flat Feet?

Flat feet, or pes planus, is a condition where the arches of the feet collapse, causing the entire sole to touch the ground when standing. It can be:

  • Flexible flatfoot: Arch appears when sitting or on tip-toes, but disappears when standing.
  • Rigid flatfoot: Arch is absent in all positions; often associated with bone abnormalities.

Studies suggest that pes planus affects a significant portion of the population and is a known contributor to lower limb pain syndromes. Source: Cleveland Clinic, 2024.

How Flat Feet Affect the Knee

The foot, ankle, and knee form a kinematic chain. When your arch drops:

  1. Overpronation: The foot rolls inward excessively.
  2. Tibial rotation: The shin bone rotates inward.
  3. Knee valgus: The knee collapses inward, stressing ligaments and cartilage.
  4. Patellar tracking issues: The kneecap may not glide smoothly, leading to anterior knee pain.

This chain reaction is well-documented in orthopedic and sports medicine literature. Source: PMC / NIH, 2024.

Common Symptoms

  • Knee pain during walking, running, or standing
  • Aching around the kneecap (patellofemoral pain)
  • Swelling after physical activity
  • Clicking or grinding sensations
  • Foot fatigue or arch pain

Treatment Options

1. Orthotics and Footwear

Custom or over-the-counter arch supports help restore alignment. Look for shoes with firm heel counters and cushioned midsoles.

2. Strengthening Exercises

Targeted exercises can reduce pronation and support the knee:

  • Short foot exercises: Lift the arch without curling toes. Hold 5 seconds, repeat 15 times. Source: SAGE Journals, 2025.
  • Calf stretches: Tight calves increase pronation. Stretch both gastrocnemius and soleus.
  • Toe yoga / toe spreads: Improves intrinsic foot muscle strength.
  • Single-leg balance: Stand on one foot for 30 seconds, progress to uneven surfaces.
  • Hip abductor strengthening: Clamshells, side leg raises — stronger hips stabilize the knee.

3. Physical Therapy

A physical therapist can assess gait, prescribe individualized exercises, and use modalities like manual therapy or taping to control pronation.

4. Anti-inflammatory Strategies

Ice, NSAIDs, and activity modification can help manage acute knee inflammation while you work on the underlying foot mechanics.

5. When Surgery Is Considered

Surgery is rarely needed for flexible flatfoot. Rigid flatfoot with severe pain or arthritis may require tendon reconstruction or joint fusion. This is always a last resort after conservative measures fail.
