Flat Feet and Knee Pain: The Connection & How to Fix It

Understanding pes planus, its impact on your knees, and evidence-based strategies for improvement

Healthy foot arch structure vs. flat foot (pes planus)

What Are Flat Feet?

Flat feet, also known as fallen arches or pes planus, is a common condition where the arch of the foot collapses, causing the entire sole to touch the ground. While many people with flat feet experience no pain, for others it can lead to a cascade of problems including foot pain, knee pain, back pain, and reduced mobility.

Key Insight: Flat feet don't just affect your feet—they change how forces travel up your entire kinetic chain, from ankles to knees, hips, and lower back.

The Flat Feet-Knee Connection

When your arch collapses, your foot rolls inward (pronation). This inward rotation travels up your leg, causing your knee to twist inward. This is called valgus stress, and it can lead to:

  • Knee pain: Especially on the inner side of the knee
  • Patellofemoral syndrome: Pain behind or around the kneecap
  • Meniscus stress: Increased wear on knee cartilage
  • Hip and back pain: From compensation patterns higher up

Causes of Flat Feet

Several factors can contribute to flat feet:

  1. Genetics: If your parents had flat feet, you're more likely to develop it
  2. Injury: Ligament or tendon damage from foot/ankle injuries
  3. Age: Tendons and ligaments weaken over time
  4. Obesity: Excess weight flattens arches under pressure
  5. Functional flat feet: Weak foot muscles that can be improved with exercise

Understanding Foot Muscles: Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic

To treat flat feet effectively, it's crucial to understand the two types of foot muscles:

Intrinsic Foot Muscles

These are the small muscles within the foot itself that directly support the arch. Think of them as your foot's "natural arch support system."

  • Located entirely within the foot
  • Control fine movements and arch stabilization
  • Can be strengthened with specific exercises

Extrinsic Foot Muscles

These are the larger muscles in the calf and shin that connect to the foot via tendons. They provide the power and major control for foot movement.

  • Tibialis Anterior: Raises the inner foot arch
  • Calf muscles (gastrocnemius, soleus): Control ankle movement and foot stability
  • Peroneal tendons: Provide lateral (outer) foot stability
  • Flexor hallucis longus & flexor digitorum longus: Deep foot control

Exercises targeting both intrinsic and extrinsic foot muscles

Treatment: Intrinsic Foot Muscle Exercises

1. Towel Scrunches (Towel Grabbing)

Target: Intrinsic foot muscles

How to do it:

  1. Sit in a chair with a smooth towel on the floor (tiles work best, not carpet)
  2. Place your foot on the towel
  3. Use your toes to scrunch the towel toward you
  4. Release and repeat 10-15 times per foot

Pro tip: The smoother the surface, the better the exercise. If your towel doesn't slide easily, the exercise loses effectiveness.

2. Marble Pick-Up / Hacky Sack

Target: Intrinsic foot muscles, toe dexterity

How to do it:

  1. Scatter marbles or a small hacky sack on the floor
  2. Pick them up using only your toes
  3. Place them in a bowl or container
  4. Repeat until you've collected all objects

Why it works: The hacky sack is actually better than marbles because it's more controlled and less frustrating. You can practice anytime—while watching TV, working at your desk, etc. It teaches your foot better movement patterns.

3. Toe Separators (Correct Toes)

Target: Intrinsic foot muscles, arch activation

How to use:

  1. Wear toe separators like Correct Toes for 15-30 minutes daily
  2. Start with short sessions—they may feel uncomfortable at first
  3. Progress to wearing them during exercises
  4. Eventually work up to wearing them during daily activities

The Science: When you spread your toes forcibly and stand up, you'll notice your arch naturally engages. Modern shoes cram our toes together, which weakens the foot muscles. Toe separators restore natural toe splay and allow your foot muscles to work properly.

Toe separators help restore natural foot alignment

Treatment: Extrinsic Foot Muscle Exercises

1. Tibialis Raises

Target: Tibialis anterior, arch lift

How to do it:

  1. Stand with feet hip-width apart
  2. Keep your toes and outer foot flat on the ground
  3. Raise your inner foot arch as high as possible
  4. Hold for 2 seconds, then lower slowly
  5. Do 1-2 sets daily, aiming for 15-20 reps per foot

Why it's important: The tibialis anterior is crucial for decelerating when your foot hits the ground. It helps absorb force and protects your knees from impact stress.

2. Calf Raises

Target: Gastrocnemius, soleus, overall foot stability

Progression:

  1. Start: Both feet, facing a wall for balance
  2. Intermediate: One foot at a time, 20 reps
  3. Advanced: Further from wall, single-leg holds

The further from the wall, the harder it is. Single-leg raises are harder than double-leg. This exercise targets most of your extrinsic foot muscles including calf muscles, flexor hallucis longus, and peroneal tendons.

Calf raises strengthen the extrinsic foot muscles

Supportive Treatments

Orthotics and Supportive Shoes

While exercises are the foundation, supportive gear can help:

  • Orthotics: Custom shoe inserts provide arch support and improve alignment
  • Supportive shoes: Look for good arch support and shock absorption
  • Avoid: Narrow shoes, worn-out shoes, or completely flat soles

Important: Orthotics are only part of the solution. If you rely on them exclusively, your foot muscles can become weaker. Think of orthotics as a crutch—helpful temporarily, but the goal is to strengthen your feet so you need them less over time.

Physical Therapy

A physical therapist can create a personalized program that combines intrinsic and extrinsic muscle strengthening, stretching, and manual therapy.

Rehabilitation for Knee Pain Associated with Flat Feet

If you're experiencing knee pain, it's important to address both the foot and knee:

  • Single-leg exercises: Build knee stability while strengthening feet
  • Don't avoid using the knee: Controlled movement and strengthening is better than complete rest
  • Gradual progression: Start with minimal pressure and build up tolerance
  • Monitor pain: Some discomfort is OK, sharp pain means stop

Lifestyle Modifications

  1. Maintain healthy weight: Reduces pressure on feet
  2. Stretch regularly: Calves, hamstrings, and Achilles tendon
  3. Rest and recovery: Ice inflamed areas, avoid aggravating activities
  4. Sunlight exposure: Important for vitamin D and overall health

Recommended Resources

Foot Collective is an excellent resource dedicated to foot strengthening and natural foot health. They offer comprehensive exercise programs and education on restoring foot function.

Your Daily Exercise Plan

Here's a simple daily routine:

  1. Morning: 2 sets of tibialis raises (15-20 reps each leg)
  2. Afternoon/Evening: 2 sets of calf raises (progress to single-leg)
  3. Anytime: Hacky sack or towel exercises while watching TV
  4. Throughout day: Wear toe separators for short periods
  5. Weekly: Gradually increase reps and difficulty

Sample Progression Timeline

  • Week 1-2: Build to 15-20 tibialis raises per leg
  • Week 3-4: Add calf raises with wall support
  • Week 5-8: Progress to single-leg calf raises
  • Month 2-3: Increase toe separator time to 1+ hours
  • Month 3+: Jogging with toe separators becomes comfortable

When to See a Doctor

Consult a healthcare professional if you experience:

  • Severe or persistent pain
  • Sudden onset of flat feet (in adulthood)
  • Significant mobility limitations
  • Knee pain limiting daily activities

Conclusion

Flat feet are common, but they don't have to limit your mobility or cause chronic pain. The key is understanding that:

  1. Foot strength is trainable: Both intrinsic and extrinsic muscles can be strengthened
  2. Consistency matters: Daily exercises, even short ones, produce results over months
  3. Knee pain is linked: Fixing your feet often resolves knee issues
  4. Holistic approach works best: Combine exercises, proper footwear, and professional guidance

Remember: recovery takes months, not days. Be patient, stay consistent, and celebrate small improvements. Your feet—and knees—will thank you.
