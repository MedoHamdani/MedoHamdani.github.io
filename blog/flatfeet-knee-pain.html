Knee Pain & Flat Feet: The Hidden Connection & How to Treat Flatfoot

Health • Biomechanics • Treatment • Updated 2025

Many people with flat feet (also called pes planus or fallen arches) focus only on foot discomfort. But the real problem often shows up somewhere else: the knee.

This guide explains exactly how flat feet and knee pain are connected, what the research shows, and what treatment options actually work — from orthotics and exercises to shoe changes and when to see a specialist.

What Are Flat Feet?

Flatfoot is a postural deformity where the medial longitudinal arch of the foot collapses. Instead of a normal curved space under the inner side of the foot, the entire sole touches or nearly touches the ground.

Normal foot arch (left) versus flat foot (right). Source: Wikimedia Commons.

Flat feet can be:

  • Flexible flatfoot — arch appears when standing on tiptoes; common in children and many adults
  • Rigid flatfoot — arch is absent in all positions; often linked to bone structure or arthritis
  • Acquired flatfoot — develops later in life, often from posterior tibial tendon dysfunction

The Biomechanical Chain: From Foot to Knee

Your body works as a linked chain. When the foot arch collapses, it sets off a chain reaction:

  1. Excessive pronation — the foot rolls inward too far during walking
  2. Internal tibial rotation — the shin bone (tibia) twists inward
  3. Knee valgus — the knee caves inward (often called "knock-knee" effect)
  4. Patellar tracking issues — kneecap doesn't glide smoothly in its groove
  5. Increased medial compartment pressure — inner knee cartilage bears more load

This is why flat feet don’t just hurt the feet — they can cause chronic knee pain, patellofemoral pain syndrome, and even early cartilage damage.

Adult flatfoot with associated ankle and knee arthritis. Source: Wikimedia Commons / Dr. V. Gaikwad.

What the Research Says

Basic knee anatomy showing the tibiofemoral and patellofemoral joints.

A NIH/PMC study found that flatfoot morphology is associated with:

  • 1.3x higher odds of frequent knee pain
  • 1.4x higher odds of medial tibiofemoral cartilage damage

Another USM study showed that the flattening foot forces the tibia to rotate internally, increasing rotational pressure on the knee joint — a direct mechanical explanation for the flat-foot-to-knee-pain pathway.

Common Symptoms

  • Pain on the inner side of the knee (medial compartment)
  • Aching after long walks or standing
  • Feet that tire quickly
  • Swelling along the arch or inner ankle
  • Difficulty standing on tiptoes
  • Back or hip pain secondary to poor alignment

Treatment Options

1. Orthotics & Arch Supports

Custom or over-the-counter orthotics are often the first line of treatment. They:

  • Support the medial longitudinal arch
  • Control excessive pronation
  • Restore more neutral limb alignment
  • Reduce abnormal knee joint loading
Custom foot orthotics help support the arch and improve alignment.

2. Physical Therapy & Exercises

Targeted exercises can strengthen the muscles that support the arch and improve knee tracking:

  • Short foot exercise — contract the arch without curling toes
  • Calf stretches — tight Achilles tendon worsens pronation
  • Toe yoga / toe spreading — activates intrinsic foot muscles
  • Single-leg balance — improves proprioception and ankle stability
  • Clamshells / hip abduction — strengthens glutes to reduce knee valgus

3. Footwear Changes

Shoes with firm midsoles, structured heel counters, and motion control features can make a big difference. Avoid completely flat soles like flip-flops or minimalist shoes if you have flat feet with knee pain.

4. Night Splints & Braces

For plantar fasciitis associated with flat feet, night splints keep the foot in a dorsiflexed position overnight, gently stretching the plantar fascia and Achilles.

5. Medical Interventions

When conservative care fails:

  • Physical therapy — manual therapy, taping, gait retraining
  • Injection therapy — corticosteroids or PRP for inflamed tendons
  • Surgery — rare, for severe rigid flatfoot or tendon ruptures

When to See a Doctor

See a podiatrist or orthopedic specialist if:

  • Knee pain lasts more than 2–3 weeks
  • You notice swelling, redness, or warmth around the knee
  • Pain limits daily activities or exercise
  • One foot arch collapsed suddenly

Prevention Tips

  1. Wear supportive shoes daily — not just for sports
  2. Do foot-strengthening exercises 3–4 times per week
  3. Maintain a healthy weight to reduce joint stress
  4. Replace worn-out running shoes regularly
  5. Stretch calves and hamstrings consistently

Summary

Flat feet and knee pain are biomechanically linked. The collapsed arch causes the tibia to rotate inward, which pushes the knee out of alignment and increases stress on the joint. The good news: most cases respond well to orthotics, targeted exercises, and smart footwear choices. Early intervention can prevent chronic knee damage and keep you active pain-free.
